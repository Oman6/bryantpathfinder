## 1. The cost of student attrition

### 1.1 Tuition revenue per FTE — what a school actually loses when a student leaves

The first input to any retention ROI is "what does one student-year of revenue look like?" The honest answer is: it varies enormously by sector, and the gross sticker number overstates the figure that actually flows to the institution.

**NCES IPEDS, postsecondary institution revenues (constant 2021-22 dollars, FY 2010–11 → FY 2020–21):**

- Public 4-year: tuition-and-fee revenue per FTE rose from $6,980 → **$8,720** (+25%).
- Private nonprofit 4-year: tuition-and-fee revenue per FTE rose from $23,330 → **$23,990** (+3%).
- Source: [NCES — Postsecondary Institution Revenues](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/cud/postsecondary-institution-revenue).

These are **net** of institutional discounts and Pell allowances at private institutions, which is the figure a CFO actually budgets against — not the published sticker price.

**NACUBO 2024 Tuition Discounting Study** (286 private nonprofits):

- Tuition discount rate: **56.3% for first-time, full-time freshmen**, **51.4% across all undergraduates** in AY 2024-25.
- Net tuition revenue rose only 1.4% in real terms last year and is projected to **decline 0.8%** in the current year.
- Sources: [NACUBO press release](https://www.nacubo.org/Press-Releases/2025/NACUBO-Study-Finds-Private-Colleges-and-Universities-Are-Offering-Record-Financial-Aid-to-Students), [NACUBO study landing page](https://www.nacubo.org/Research/2025/NACUBO-Tuition-Discounting-Study), [Inside Higher Ed coverage](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/business/revenue-strategies/2025/06/24/tuition-discounting-hits-another-high).

**Implication for Bryant.** Bryant University is private nonprofit, AACSB-accredited, with ~3,800 undergraduates. Bryant's published tuition is roughly $54K and total cost of attendance ~$78K (publicly available rates), but the **net** tuition revenue per FTE is almost certainly in the $24K–$30K range after discounting — much closer to the IPEDS private-nonprofit median than to the sticker number. A pitch to Bryant should use net tuition revenue, not gross.

### 1.2 The standard 1% retention-lift formula

The widely cited Ruffalo Noel Levitz framework treats attrition cost as the lost margin from a student who would otherwise have continued generating tuition revenue for the remaining years of their program. The arithmetic is simple but disciplined:

```
Annual NTR per FTE × Years of remaining enrollment × Number of students retained
= Marginal revenue from a 1pp retention lift
```

**Worked example for a 10,000-student private 4-year (representative of a regional comprehensive):**

- 10,000 undergraduates × 1 percentage point retention lift = **100 additional retained students/year**
- 100 students × ~3 years of remaining average enrollment × $24,000 net tuition = **~$7.2M in cumulative tuition revenue retained per cohort**
- On an annualized basis (current year only): 100 × $24K = **$2.4M/year**
- At a public flagship using $8,720 NTR/FTE: 100 × $8,720 ≈ **$872K/year** in current-year tuition alone.

**The Georgia State data point gives a real-world calibration.** GSU publicly states that *every percentage point increase in graduation rate is worth ~$3M/year in tuition and fees* at their ~33K-student scale. That's ~$90/FTE/percentage-point — a useful sanity check ([EAB / GSU partner story](https://eab.com/why-eab/partner-stories/how-georgia-state-university-used-data-driven-interventions-to-add-3m-in-tuition-revenue/), [EWA reporting](https://ewa.org/news-explainers/how-georgia-state-dramatically-changed-its-graduation-rate-and-how-other-universities-can-too)).

### 1.3 Replacement cost — the *other* half of the attrition bill

When a student leaves, the school pays twice: lost forward tuition AND the marginal cost of recruiting a replacement.

- **Median cost to recruit one undergraduate (2017 figures, RNL):** **$2,357 at private institutions, $536 at publics**. Source: [RNL 2018 Cost of Recruiting Report](http://learn.ruffalonl.com/rs/395-EOG-977/images/RNL_2018_Cost_of_Recruiting_Report_EM-005.pdf). Inflation-adjusted to 2026, those figures are roughly $3,000 (private) and $700 (public).
- The 2022 RNL update is consistent with this band: [RNL 2022 Cost of Recruiting Report](https://learn.ruffalonl.com/rs/395-EOG-977/images/RNL_2022_CostRecruiting_Report.pdf).

So the all-in cost of one student leaving a private 4-year mid-program is approximately:

```
1 lost student-year × ~$24K NTR
+ ~$3K replacement marketing/admissions cost
+ unrecovered fixed-cost amortization (housing/food-service contracts, course over-staffing)
= ~$27K–$32K per departure, conservatively
```

Multiply by typical attrition: at a 75% retention rate, a 4,000-student private school loses ~1,000 students/year (combining graduation and attrition). If even ~250 of those are non-graduating departures, the institutional bleed is **~$7M/year** before considering replacement marketing.

## 2. Time-to-degree and the cost of an extra semester

### 2.1 The 4-year vs. 6-year gap

NCES Fast Facts 40 ([source](https://nces.ed.gov/fastfacts/display.asp?id=40)) and the Condition of Education ([source](https://nces.ed.gov/programs/coe/indicator/ctr)):

- Fall 2010 cohort, first-time/full-time bachelor's seekers at 4-year institutions: **41% graduated in 4 years; 56% in 5 years; 60% in 6 years**.
- Fall 2014 cohort: **64% 6-year graduation rate**, with public = 63%, private nonprofit = 68%, private for-profit = 29%.
- Female 6-year rate: 67%. Male 6-year rate: 60%.

Translation: ~**half of all 4-year-college bachelor's graduates take more than 4 years**. Each additional semester costs the student tuition and lost wage-earning years, and costs the institution none of the marginal revenue (it's a wash for tuition) but a real reputational hit on published 4-year rates that increasingly factor into US News rankings, Title IV oversight, and outcomes-based state formulas.

### 2.2 The "swirl" / excess credits problem

Complete College America has been the loudest voice on this:

- Associate-degree earners typically accumulate **22 excess credits** — three-quarters of an additional academic year. Community-college transfers to 4-year schools accumulate **~29 excess credits**. Source: [Hechinger Report on Complete College America data](https://hechingerreport.org/wasted-time-money-undergraduate-classes/).
- A Greater Texas Foundation study found excess credits cost students and Texas taxpayers **$120M/year in that state alone**.
- CCA's "[15 to Finish](https://completecollege.org/strategy/15-to-finish/)" campaign quantifies the credit-load problem: students taking 12 credits a semester rather than 15 are mathematically locked into >4 years.
- See also [Inside Higher Ed on excess-credit-hour policies](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/2017/07/20/excess-credit-hour-policies-increase-student-debt) and [Florida's excess credit surcharge](https://www.floridashines.org/excess-credit-surcharge), which charges students out-of-state rates after exceeding 110% of program credits — a direct, dollar-quantified policy response.

**Per-student cost of one extra semester:**

- Private 4-year: ~$12K (one-semester net tuition) + opportunity cost of delayed earnings (~$25–35K, BLS median bachelor's starting wage)
- Public 4-year in-state: ~$5K direct + $25–35K opportunity cost
- Combined societal cost per unnecessary semester: $30K–$50K per student

**Where Pathfinder fits.** The product's primary daily value is helping a student avoid registering for a course that won't apply. That is precisely the swirl problem CCA has been measuring for a decade. Pathfinder doesn't move the dial on a 6-year graduation rate at the institution level in year one — but it can plausibly reduce excess credits at the student level immediately, and that's a story a Provost recognizes.

## 3. Advisor labor — caseloads, salaries, and routine time-sink

### 3.1 Caseload ratios

NACADA's most-cited figure (from the 2011 National Survey of Academic Advising — still used today because no superseding national survey exists at the same scope):

- **Median caseload: 296 students per full-time professional advisor.**
- By institution type: 441 (2-year), 260 (public bachelor's), 100 (private bachelor's), 300 (public master's), 179 (private master's), 285 (public doctorate), 200 (private doctorate).
- NACADA explicitly does **not** recommend a target ratio.
- Source: [NACADA Clearinghouse — Advisor to Student Ratio Resources](https://nacada.ksu.edu/Resources/Clearinghouse/View-Articles/Advisor-to-Student-Ratio-Caseload-Resources.aspx), [Advisor Load article](https://nacada.ksu.edu/Resources/Clearinghouse/View-Articles/Advisor-Load).

**Caveats.** The 2011 number is widely repeated but stale; anecdotally many R1 caseloads have ballooned to 400–600+. A pitch deck claim of "advisor caseloads are 1:300+" is defensible. A claim of "1:600 at large publics" needs the qualifier "at some R1s, per advisor surveys."

### 3.2 Advisor compensation

BLS Occupational Outlook Handbook, "School and Career Counselors and Advisors" (SOC 21-1012):

- **Median annual wage (May 2024): $65,140.** 10th percentile $43,580; 90th percentile $105,870.
- Hourly median: $31.32.
- Source: [BLS OOH](https://www.bls.gov/ooh/community-and-social-service/school-and-career-counselors.htm), [BLS OEWS detailed table](https://www.bls.gov/oes/2023/may/oes211012.htm).

Loaded cost (benefits, employer-side taxes, ~30% loading): **~$85K/year/FTE advisor**.

### 3.3 Time spent on routine schedule troubleshooting — what we can honestly say

There is no first-class published time-and-motion study breaking out "minutes per week per advisor spent on schedule conflicts and registration troubleshooting" that I can cite. What we *do* have:

- Stellic's Columbia SPS case study reports advisors **reduced graduation-audit time from 40 hours to 4 hours per audit cycle** ([source](https://www.stellic.com/customer-story/columbia-university-school-of-professional-studies)). This is a degree-audit metric, not a registration-conflict metric, but it's the closest published comparable.
- EAB's published advising research suggests "just-in-time" interventions on routine questions can free meaningful advisor capacity, but EAB's specific minute-counts are behind their member paywall ([EAB advising resources](https://eab.com/resources/research-report/meet-student-readiness-gaps-just-in-time-advising/)).
- Inside Higher Ed reporting on advisor capacity gaps ([source](https://www.insidehighered.com/news/student-success/academic-life/2023/02/28/student-survey-reveals-gaps-core-academic-advising)) supports the directional claim that routine procedural advising crowds out developmental/career advising.

**Defensible estimate (label as estimate in pitch material):** Of an advisor's ~40-hour week, ~6–10 hours during registration-heavy weeks (typically 4 weeks per semester) are spent on routine schedule-conflict and "what should I take" questions. At $85K loaded, that is roughly $200–$330/week of advisor time per advisor per registration week, or **~$1,200–$2,600/advisor/semester** of capacity that is being spent on the lowest-judgment work an advisor does. For a school with 30 advisors that is **$36K–$78K/semester** in reclaimable capacity if the routine layer is automated. This is an estimate built on BLS wages × directional time fractions — not a citable industry figure. Owen should label it as such.

## 4. Published lift from comparable tools — strong claims, weak claims, vendor claims

This is the most contested section, because vendors have strong incentives to claim large lifts. Below: each vendor's headline number, the methodology rigor I could verify, and what a buyer should weight it at.

### 4.1 EAB Navigate — Georgia State

- **Claim:** GSU raised its 6-year graduation rate by **23 percentage points** since 2003 and is graduating ~2,000 more students/year. Every percentage point ≈ **$3M/year** in additional tuition/fees.
- Source: [EAB partner story](https://eab.com/why-eab/partner-stories/how-georgia-state-university-used-data-driven-interventions-to-add-3m-in-tuition-revenue/); independent reporting at [EWA](https://ewa.org/news-explainers/how-georgia-state-dramatically-changed-its-graduation-rate-and-how-other-universities-can-too) and [Inside Higher Ed](https://www.insidehighered.com/digital-learning/article/2017/07/19/georgia-state-improves-student-outcomes-data).
- **Methodology rigor:** Multi-year, large-N, partially confounded with parallel changes (advising restructuring, completion-grant program, summer-melt chatbot, Banner-tied early-alert system). The 23-point lift is **not** attributable to Navigate alone. EAB's own marketing usually says "since GSU started working with EAB" — the school is honest that EAB was one of several interventions.
- **What a buyer should believe:** Directionally yes, structured advising + data integration helps. Specifically attributing 3% of GSU's 23-point gain to EAB software is unsupported by published evaluation studies.

### 4.2 Civitas Learning — published Outcomes / Impact Reports

- **Slippery Rock University:** **3% retention increase**, "highest rate since 2004." [Source](https://www.civitaslearning.com/customer-success-stories/slippery-rock-university/).
- **UTSA:** **4.4 percentage point persistence lift** for students who engaged with academic advising; **1.6 pp** for proactive nudges to academic coaching. [Source](https://www.civitaslearning.com/customer-success-stories/how-utsa-sustained-enrollment-with-data-activated-retention-strategies/).
- **Austin Community College:** Fall-to-spring persistence rose from 70.8% (Spring 2021) to 74.9% (Spring 2023) — **4.1 percentage points over 3 years**. [Source](https://www.civitaslearning.com/customer-success-stories/austin-community-college-persistence/).
- **Methodology rigor:** Civitas does publish quasi-experimental analyses and treatment-vs-control comparisons in their [Student Impact Reports](https://studentimpactreport.civitaslearning.com/2025) and [Outcomes archive](https://www.civitaslearning.com/outcomes/). This is the most analytically rigorous vendor in the category. Still self-published; not peer-reviewed.
- **Independent perspective:** The Higher Ed Dive piece "[40% of student success initiatives don't help retention](https://www.highereddive.com/news/report-40-of-student-success-initiatives-dont-help-retention/562712/)" is itself based on Civitas's own platform analysis — *they* are the ones reporting that most interventions don't work, which lends credibility to the ones that do.

### 4.3 Stellic — degree planning

- **Wisconsin-Stout:** 64% student adoption, 75% advisor adoption in 6 months; 99% of online-students bring transfer credit accounted for in plans. [Source](https://www.stellic.com/customer-story/university-of-wisconsin-stout-2).
- **Columbia SPS:** Graduation-audit time **40 hours → 4 hours**. [Source](https://www.stellic.com/customer-story/columbia-university-school-of-professional-studies).
- **Methodology rigor:** Operational metrics (adoption, time-saved), not retention/graduation outcomes. Honest framing: Stellic publishes process-improvement claims, not outcome claims.
- **Lesson for BryantPathfinder pitching:** This is the playbook. Don't promise a graduation-rate lift; promise time-back, accuracy, adoption. Those metrics are visible in 90 days.

### 4.4 Mainstay (formerly AdmitHub) — Georgia State / Pounce

- **Summer melt reduction:** **22% in 2016**, grown to **37%** since. [Source](https://mainstay.com/case-study/how-georgia-state-university-uses-behavioral-intelligence-to-improve-student-retention-and-persistence/).
- **Treatment-effect on June-1-committed students:** 3.3% enrollment increase, **21.4% reduction in summer melt**.
- **Persistence:** A randomized controlled trial (Page, Castleman, et al.) showed **3 pp lift in FAFSA filing and re-registration**; 50% reduction in withdrawals due to outstanding balances. [Source](https://success.gsu.edu/initiatives/reduction-of-summer-melt/).
- **Methodology rigor:** This is the **only** product in the category with a peer-published RCT — the Castleman/Page summer melt study is the gold standard. Mainstay's published numbers track well to the academic literature.
- **Implication:** RCT-validated outcomes are the bar for "real" claims. No course-scheduling tool currently has one.

### 4.5 What a synthesis honestly says about the category

Every vendor claims 2–6 percentage points of lift on retention or persistence. Most of those claims are based on partner-school case studies with confounded interventions. Only Mainstay has an RCT. This is the credibility gap a Provost will probe — and the right response from BryantPathfinder is to **not claim retention lift in year one** and instead claim measurable, immediate process metrics (registration time, schedule errors, advisor-touch reduction).

## 5. The higher-ed budget calendar — when can a deal actually close?

### 5.1 Fiscal year structure

- **~95% of U.S. universities run a July 1 – June 30 fiscal year** (the rest are mostly Sept 1 – Aug 31 or calendar-year).
- See examples: [Princeton Budget Cycle Calendar](https://finance.princeton.edu/budgeting-financial-management/budgeting-and-planning/budget-cycle/budget-cycle-calendar), [University of Michigan Budget Calendar](https://obp.umich.edu/budget/budget-calendar/), [ODU Fiscal Year-End Deadlines](https://www.odu.edu/finance/controllers-office/year-end-deadlines).

### 5.2 Month-by-month, where a vendor is in the conversation

| Month | What's happening on campus | What a vendor does |
|---|---|---|
| **July** | New fiscal year starts; new operating budgets unlocked | Send introductions, schedule fall meetings; this is when discretionary line items have fresh dollars |
| **August** | Pre-semester rush; new student onboarding | Bad time to pitch operationally, but good time to have product visible during registration crunch |
| **September** | Fall semester opens; pain is acute | **Best window for a "see-the-pain" demo** — registration just happened, advisors are exhausted |
| **October** | Procurement evaluates fall RFPs; pilot funds visible | Begin formal pilot conversations; respond to RFPs |
| **November** | Provost-level FY+1 planning kicks off | Position for inclusion in next-year budget asks |
| **December** | Holiday slowdown; year-end purchasing for some calendar-year orgs | Send proposals before break; close any one-time-fund deals |
| **January** | Spring semester chaos repeats | Demo if pain is fresh; otherwise quiet |
| **February** | **Budget hearings; Provost finalizes asks to CFO/Board** | Critical: this is when *your* line item has to be in the deck |
| **March–April** | Board of Trustees approves FY+1 budget | Too late to add a line item. Pivot to one-time funds and pilots |
| **May** | Year-end purchasing; "use it or lose it" departmental dollars | **Second-best window for opportunistic small-dollar deals** |
| **June** | Fiscal year-end close; spending freeze | Quiet. Plan for July restart |

### 5.3 RFP timing

- RFPs typically go out in **late summer to early fall** (Aug–Oct) for the *current* fiscal year, or in **late spring** (April–May) for the *upcoming* fiscal year.
- Drafting + review + negotiation cycle is **3–6 months minimum**. Realistically 6–9 months at large institutions. Sources: [E&I Cooperative — Guide to RFP Contracts](https://www.eandi.org/resources/ei-blog/guide-rfp-contracts-higher-ed/), [Agile Education Marketing — RFP Guide](https://agile-ed.com/resources/rfp-for-schools-a-complete-guide-for-vendors-to-win-more-bids/).

### 5.4 How to close a deal *outside* the budget cycle

- **One-time funds.** Provost discretionary lines, dean's strategic funds, "innovation" buckets. Often $10K–$50K, no procurement-committee involvement, faster sign-off.
- **Pilot money.** Many institutions explicitly carve out small pilot budgets (often $5K–$25K) to evaluate vendors before the next-year RFP cycle.
- **Student technology fee.** At many publics, the student tech fee committee (often student-vote-controlled) has its own ~$50K–$500K annual budget for student-facing tools. Cycle is usually **fall vote → spring allocation**.
- **Grant-funded pilots.** Title III/V, Lumina, Strada, ECMC grants can fund a pilot deployment without touching the operating budget at all (see Section 6).

### 5.5 The right time to be in a Provost's inbox — for Owen specifically

Owen is solo, has one warm institution (Bryant), and his graduation date is May 2029. His sales calendar should be:

- **Now (April–May 2026, end of sophomore year):** Lock down a sanctioned, opt-in, no-money Bryant pilot for Fall 2026 registration (May–June 2026 registration window). This is **not a sales motion**; it is a pilot that creates a published outcome to use later.
- **September–October 2026:** Demo the Bryant pilot results at one or two regional NACADA chapter meetings or a Coursedog/Stellic-adjacent conference. Use the pilot to start non-Bryant Provost conversations.
- **November 2026 – February 2027:** Position to be in Provosts' **FY28 budget hearings** (Feb 2027). This is the realistic earliest moment a non-Bryant institution writes a check.
- **April–May 2027 (junior year end):** Year-end one-time-fund conversations. Realistic small-dollar pilots ($5K–$15K) at 1–2 schools.
- **September 2027 onward (senior year):** RFP-responsive selling for FY29.

This means **the first paid non-Bryant deal is realistically ~14 months out from now**, and that's the optimistic line. The mistake to avoid: pitching a Provost in March–May, when budgets are locked. They will be polite and never email back.

For Pathfinder, this still matters because in PBF states, *Provosts care more about the metrics Pathfinder claims to influence* — even if academically the lift is debated.

**Practical priority order for Pathfinder pilots:**

1. Student technology fee (year 1, easiest entry, student-facing fit)
2. Provost discretionary one-time funds (year 1, $5K–$25K)
3. Operating budget student-success line (year 2+, FY28 cycle)
4. Title III/V grants (year 2+, only at MSI/HSI/HBCU-eligible institutions)
5. Foundation grants (year 2+, slow, large)

## 7. One-page ROI calculator — template for a pitch deck or spreadsheet

The following is the calculator the synthesis agent should turn into a spreadsheet. All inputs are knobs the school can change; all outputs are dollarized.

### Inputs

| Input | Default | Source for default |
|---|---|---|
| Total undergraduate FTE | 4,000 | Bryant-scale |
| Net tuition revenue / FTE | $24,000 | NCES + NACUBO discount-adjusted private nonprofit |
| Average years remaining for current students | 3.0 | NCES bachelor's cohort data |
| Current 6-year graduation rate | 64% | NCES national |
| Current 4-year graduation rate | 41% | NCES national |
| Average advisors employed | 15 | NACADA 1:300 ratio |
| Loaded advisor cost / FTE | $85,000 | BLS + 30% loading |
| Hours/advisor/registration-week on routine scheduling | 8 | Estimate (label as such) |
| Registration-heavy weeks per year | 8 | 4/semester × 2 semesters |
| Average excess credits per graduate | 12 | Conservative midpoint of CCA's range |
| Marginal credit cost (school-side) | $400 | Sector midpoint |

### Outputs (per year)

```
Tuition retained from 1pp retention lift
  = FTE × 0.01 × NTR
  = 4,000 × 0.01 × $24,000 = $960,000

Tuition retained over remaining-years horizon (3 yr)
  = $960,000 × 3 = $2,880,000

Replacement-cost avoided (1pp retention)
  = FTE × 0.01 × $3,000 (private recruit cost)
  = $120,000

Advisor capacity reclaimed (50% efficiency on routine work)
  = Advisors × hours/wk × weeks × hourly loaded rate × 50%
  = 15 × 8 × 8 × ($85,000 / 2,080 hrs) × 0.5
  = ~$19,600/year of recovered advisor capacity

Excess-credit reduction (3 credits/graduate × 1,000 graduates × $400)
  = $1,200,000 of mis-applied institutional capacity per cohort

Total year-1 conservative impact (just advisor + replacement, no retention claim)
  = ~$140,000

Total year-3 cumulative if 1pp retention lift achieved
  = ~$3.0M
```

### Pricing implication

If Pathfinder lists at **$15,000–$30,000/yr** for a 4,000-FTE school, the **payback period is <12 months on advisor-capacity savings alone** — even if zero retention lift is claimed. That's the honest pitch.

## 8. What Owen can honestly claim — the strong/weak/dishonest taxonomy

This is the most important section because the long-term moat for a solo founder is credibility, and overclaiming will close more doors than it opens.

### Strong claims (defensible from first principles + product facts on the ground)

- **"Pathfinder eliminates time-conflicted schedules. The deterministic solver cannot return a conflicted schedule, period."** Verifiable by inspection of the codebase + ADR 0003. This is a true product-correctness claim.
- **"Pathfinder generates three valid candidate schedules in under 2 seconds end-to-end."** Measured. True today.
- **"Pathfinder reduces routine scheduling questions to advisors during registration weeks."** Defensible directionally; quantifiable post-pilot via advisor time-survey or ticket-system delta.
- **"Pathfinder helps students avoid registering for courses that don't apply to their degree."** Verifiable per session — the requirement-expander only surfaces sections that satisfy unmet requirements. This is the swirl/excess-credits angle.

### Weak claims (true in spirit but not yet measurable from a small pilot)

- **"Pathfinder will improve the 4-year graduation rate by X percentage points."** Sample size, time horizon, and confounders make this unprovable from a year-one pilot. Don't make this claim.
- **"Pathfinder will save advisors X hours per week."** Directionally yes, but the specific number requires an advisor time-and-motion study. Frame as "we will measure this in the pilot" rather than "we know this is the answer."
- **"Pathfinder will reduce excess credits."** Plausible mechanism; small pilot won't show statistically. Frame as outcome-of-interest for multi-year deployment.

### Dishonest claims (don't make them, ever)

- **"Pathfinder will save $X million per year"** — at zero deployments, this is fiction.
- **"Schools using Pathfinder see a Y% retention lift"** — there is no plural "schools" and there is no retention data. Don't attempt the EAB/Civitas-style aggregate-claim playbook until there is real data behind it.
- **"Pathfinder is FERPA-compliant out of the box"** — see Section 5 of the product baseline. Audit content goes through the Anthropic API today; without an executed school-official agreement and data-handling controls, FERPA compliance is a roadmap item, not a current state.
- **"Pathfinder integrates with Banner/Workday/PeopleSoft"** — the current state is one-way ingestion via screenshot/text. Until there is a real Ellucian/Workday integration, this claim is false.

### The honest year-one elevator pitch

> "Course scheduling is the most-trafficked, least-strategic interaction between students and advisors at every U.S. university. BryantPathfinder turns it into a 90-second self-service flow, returns three valid conflict-free schedules with professor and workload context, and gives the advisor back the registration-week capacity they spend on triage. Year one: we measure registration time saved, student satisfaction, and routine ticket reduction. Years two and three, when there is enough deployment to support the analysis, we measure excess-credit reduction and time-to-degree. We do not promise a graduation-rate lift on a small pilot — that's not credible from anyone, and the fact that we won't make that claim is the reason you should consider us."

That sentence is the entire ROI pitch. Everything in this document supports it.
